Add a dark theme

The case for offering a dark theme. Hand-authored with no tooling, and deliberately carrying no semantic units at all: a proposal argues value and need, and this document exists partly to demonstrate that prose alone conforms.

Why

Readers work in dark rooms and on dark desktops, and a page that ignores that is a page they squint at or leave. Every operating system this application runs on now reports a preference, so the information is available and unused.

The cost of not doing it compounds quietly. Each new page adds colors to a palette nobody is auditing, and a theme added later has to reconcile all of them at once instead of a handful.

What would change

The application would offer two themes and remember which one the reader chose. With no choice recorded, it would follow what the operating system reports rather than assuming light.

Both themes would have to be legible rather than merely dark. That means a measured contrast floor for every text and background pair, checked by the suite rather than by eye — a dark theme that looks finished and fails contrast is worse than none, because it looks like the problem was solved.

Alternatives considered

Shipping only a dark theme and dropping the light one was the simplest option, and it trades one group of unhappy readers for another. A preference the reader controls costs one control and one stored value.

Following the operating system with no override at all was the next simplest. It fails the reader whose system preference and reading preference differ, which is common enough on shared machines to be worth the control.

Scope

A per-page theme override is out of scope. It multiplies the pairs a contrast check must cover, and no reader has asked for it.

Nothing above is a requirement yet. This document argues that the change is worth making; what the application must actually do is settled in the specification, and stating it here would be claiming a contract at the stage meant for arguing about one.
